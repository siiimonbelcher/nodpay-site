Why can't my bookkeeper make payments from our Starling business account?

Short answer: because Starling doesn't have a role for them. Business accounts give full access to directors and people with significant control — and nobody else. There's no bookkeeper seat, no payment-preparer role, no approval workflow. If payroll needs paying, a director does it.

Why Starling works this way

It's a deliberate security position, and it's not unreasonable: most payment fraud against small companies involves someone other than the owner touching the bank. By keeping payment authority locked to directors, Starling keeps the blast radius small. The trouble is that the same rule ignores how real companies run — where an accounts person, finance manager or external bookkeeper prepares payroll, and the director's only sensible job is a final look and a yes.

What people try instead (and why it goes wrong)

  • Sharing the director's login or phone. Breaks Starling's terms, destroys the audit trail, and hands unrestricted payment power to someone you only meant to give payroll duty. This is the workaround fraud investigators find at the bottom of most horror stories.
  • The director does payroll manually. The default. Every payday, someone who delegated payroll re-enters the process anyway — retyping amounts on a Friday, or fiddling with Starling's bulk-payment CSV. Works, and everyone quietly resents it.
  • Changing banks. Some banks offer delegate roles — but if you like Starling (many do), moving your banking to fix one workflow is a big hammer for a small nail.
  • Prefunded payroll wallets. Some payment platforms have the company top up an e-money account the bookkeeper can pay from. It works, but your payroll money now sits outside your bank, and the subscriptions typically run £25–£50 a month per company.

The fix: split the authority, not the bank account

The organisational model companies actually want isn't "give the bookkeeper bank access" — it's a split: the finance person gets authority to prepare; the director keeps authority to approve. Starling's restriction stays intact — nobody but you can move money — but the work stops boomeranging back to your desk.
